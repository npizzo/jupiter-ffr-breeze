Why is the anelastic no-jet flow more zonal?

Both no-jet cases start with the same velocity field and negligible zonal mean. Neither has momentum relaxation. Their subsequent mean flows separate:

No-jet caseZonal energy share, t=1000At t=2000Mid-depth zonal velocity RMS, t=2000
Compressible32.8%45.0%0.0973
Anelastic87.5%78.4%0.1828

Energy share uses the zonal component of the reference-density-weighted depth-mean velocity, divided by that depth-mean velocity's total kinetic energy—not total three-dimensional energy. Zonal averaging also captures part of a localized vortex.

By t=250 the shares are already 33.8% versus 78.4%. The compressible no-jet lower cap first develops negative horizontally averaged N² much later, around t=1353. Cap erosion alone therefore cannot explain the onset.

The anelastic calculation constrains mass flux using fixed stratified reference density; it is not unstratified incompressible flow. The compressible calculation evolves density and its background stratification. At t=2000 its no-jet mean-density profile differs locally by up to 191% from its initial profile. Maximum temperature-profile departure is about 45% versus 16%. The lower compressible cap becomes unstable, whereas the anelastic mean cap remains stable.

Interpretation: these have become different thermal states, not a clean isolation of acoustic compressibility. Different momentum transport, mass redistribution and effective numerical dissipation are possible contributors. Without a closed zonal momentum budget or seed-to-seed comparison, the dominant cause is unresolved.

The cap-jet runs have an additional distinction: their momentum relaxation acts throughout the column, toward zero below the cap and toward the target jet above it. It can suppress self-generated mean flow. It cannot explain the difference between the two no-jet cases, which have no such relaxation.

Why this is not yet a Chan & Mayr reproduction

Chan & Mayr (2013) found a dominant anticyclone with a smaller cyclone in their Ω=3, 67.5°N case. A surviving cyclone does not alone contradict their result. Their setup differs materially:

IngredientChan & MayrCurrent suite
Horizontal domain4×4, doubly periodic, constant f4×8 channel, north/south walls, β
Cap coolingLocal temperature relaxationHorizontal-mean-only relaxation
NumericsConservative total energy; subgrid viscosity and thermal diffusionPotential-temperature formulation; WENO9, no explicit closure
Grid540×540×80128×256×80: about 4.2 times coarser horizontal spacing
Thermal evolutionRelaxation times exceeding 10⁴t=2000, substantial thermal drift

Ideal-gas constants, gravity, nominal cap interface, heat flux, Ω and latitude are similar. But Ω alone does not establish their anticyclonic regime. Their Rossby number uses measured nonrotating convective RMS velocity; our imposed initial eddy RMS of 0.136 is not that measurement. Their Ω=3 case at 22.5°N was cyclonic, so moving toward GRS latitude does not automatically produce their anticyclones.

A periodic constant-f benchmark of convection-driven sign selection and a β-channel/jet study of vortex persistence answer different questions. These four runs explore the latter, with substantial thermal caveats. They should not be described as a successful or failed reproduction of Chan's benchmark.

Why were tracers hard to see?

They are nearly uniform. The lower anelastic cap-jet panel has RMS concentration variation only 1.14×10⁻⁶ at t=2000, versus 0.025 initially. The former display hid that range. Enhanced contrast reveals remaining patterns without implying large concentration differences. Homogenization includes numerical diffusion as well as stirring; this is not a calibrated molecular-mixing measurement.

A separate fidelity limitation remains: compressible thermal-energy accounting residuals are about −29% of supplied bottom heat over the full run. This is a budget residual, not an established physical cooling mechanism. The analogous anelastic compressible-energy proxy is not a conserved-energy diagnostic.
